----
title: "my thoughts on mathematics"
date: "20-06-2026"
----
i have been a student of physics, chemistry, biology and mathematics for almost the past 15 years of my life, and if there's one conviction that's survived all of it unshaken, it's this: nothing i have ever put my eyes on has been free of math. i have watched wars unfold on a screen, lived through a pandemic, watched messi finally lift a world cup after two decades of near misses — and the thing that gave every single one of those moments its weight, its gravity, was numbers. casualty counts. R-naught values nobody understood but everybody quoted. a man's goal tally measured against another man's goal tally like it was the only argument that mattered. i am, genuinely, not interested in anything that doesn't have numbers attached to it, because i don't know how else to measure the size of a thing. how big was the war. how bad was the wave. how good was the goat. you tell me the number, and suddenly i can hold the thing in my hand. the only place i'll grudgingly admit numbers might not belong is love — and even then, i'd ask you to remember a few dates on the calendar before you get too romantic about it.
there's also a quieter, less dramatic version of this everywhere-ness that i think about a lot. your heartbeat is a number. the price of your coffee is a number doing a small violent dance with inflation. the song you have on loop right now is built out of frequencies and time signatures and counts of four. the code running this website is, underneath all of it, just numbers pretending to be language. you don't need a war or a world cup to find math — you just need to stop assuming it isn't there.
and yet — there has been, i think, a kind of generational hatred quietly insinuated into students about math. somewhere along the way, liking it got rebranded as a personality disorder. the kids who were good at it got cast as some strange subspecies staying away from the sun, scattered hair, a vitamin D deficiency, allergic to small talk. honestly, it was never that serious. math has always felt to me like a kind of music that never quite satiates the brain — you solve one thing and it just opens a door to three more rooms you didn't know existed. i've never been able to take pride in someone being "good at math" the way people take pride in, say, memorizing every line of a film, because math isn't a trick you learn. it's closer to a language the brain was already fluent in before anyone got around to teaching it formally. and if you want proof of how natural it is, ask yourself why one of humanity's greatest inventions was music. music is just math wearing a nicer outfit. the same ratios that make an octave sound "right" to a baby who has never heard the word "ratio" are doing actual arithmetic in real time, and nobody told the baby to do that math, it just happened. i could spend an entire separate piece on how rhythm is just subdivision, how harmony is just frequency ratios behaving themselves, how a time signature is basically a fraction with a drumbeat — but i'm not here to turn anyone into a music-theory nerd today. just know the door is there if you want to walk through it later.
what i actually want to talk about is the strange, lurching way you're introduced to numbers as you grow up — because nobody warns you it happens in stages, and each stage quietly rewires how big you think the universe is allowed to be.
stage 1: 1, 2, 3, 4…
this is the starter pack. you count fingers, you count stairs, you count days until your birthday. the world is made of whole, countable, well-behaved things, and for a while that feels like the whole truth. there is a kind of innocence to this stage that you don't get back — everything is additive, everything makes sense, nothing has betrayed you yet.
stage 2: zero, and the number line
and then someone hands you zero, and the number line tips over and grows a mirror image of itself going the other way, and your world quietly flips on its axis. this is the stage where kids start mugging up rules about what happens when you multiply a negative by a positive, what happens when you divide one negative by another — rules that feel arbitrary until, years later, they suddenly don't. and then there's zero itself. god knows the number of strange little theories students cook up about it and go bother their teachers with. zero is the only number with no real-world referent you can point to and hold — you can show someone three apples, you cannot show someone zero apples, you can only show them the absence of apples and ask them to believe you. that's also, weirdly, the only number people seem to be scared of, whether it's sitting in their bank balance or hovering somewhere in the back of their mind about their own life. can zero be a stand-in for the idea of god — something defined entirely by what it isn't? i don't know. but it's the first time math asks you to believe in something you can't see, and that's not a small thing to ask of a nine-year-old.
stage 3: squares, cubes, and roots
this stage comes in two halves, good news and bad news. squares and cubes are sunshine and rainbows — multiply a number by itself, watch it grow, feel powerful. roots are the darkest thunderstorm you'll meet in a textbook so far. this, i genuinely believe, is where a lot of students quietly give up on math, somewhere around the moment they're asked to find the cube root of some nine-digit monstrosity on a monday morning, first bench, no calculator in sight. and sure, people love to ask why we even bother teaching this when calculators and supercomputers exist. i'd ask back: why do buildings still have stairs when there are elevators? if you never learn to walk, how exactly are you supposed to learn to fly? this is also, not coincidentally, the stage where the number line stops being a line and reveals itself as an axis — the X-axis — and big, intimidating names like the "cartesian plane," courtesy of monsieur rené descartes, enter the chat. roots crack the door open to irrational and rational numbers, an entire continent of things that can exist, refuse to exist, or insist on coexisting whether you like it or not. and the single most satisfying moment in this whole stage, at least for me, was using pythagoras' theorem — yes, that pythagoras, the triangle guy — to physically locate an irrational number on the number line. proof that something which technically "can't be written down exactly" still has an exact, defendable address. that one stuck with me.
stage 4: complex numbers
the problem invented for no reason. or maybe every reason. complex numbers are the thing every engineering student wrestles with for the better part of a degree, and the longer you sit with them the more genuinely absurd they become — how a number that was originally just a workaround, a hack to let square roots of negative numbers exist on paper, ends up running the entire backstage of electrical engineering and signal processing. this is the stage where you start being formally introduced to the names that show up on coins and currency and university buildings — euler, gauss, fibonacci — people who didn't just play with numbers but built entire load-bearing walls of modern science out of them.
now, all of this — and i mean genuinely all of it — is maybe 20% of what's actually out there, and even that 20% is mostly confined to arithmetic and its more dramatic cousins. the stage that actually broke most students i know, the one that decides whether someone walks away calling themselves a "math person" or not, hasn't even shown up yet: calculus, courtesy of one isaac newton, arguably the greatest scientist to ever do it. that one's too close to my heart to rush through here, so i'll save it for a piece of its own.
for now, i'll just leave you with this: next time you watch a war update scroll past in numbers, or argue about whether a footballer is the greatest of all time using nothing but stats, or hum a song without realizing you're doing fractions with your foot — that's not math intruding on life. that's just what life looks like once you notice the numbers were never not there.
